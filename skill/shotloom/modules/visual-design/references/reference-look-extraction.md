# Learn from a visual reference without inventing its recipe

Use when translating an inspected reference, tutorial or canvas example into a proposed look. This is not a requirement to research every shot or to adopt the reference's medium. A prompt or workflow graph documents intent and operations, not successful visible execution; label unviewed media accordingly.

## Four separate evidence layers

1. **Observed appearance:** describe only what the inspected image/range supports: silhouette, edge treatment, shadow boundaries, local color, material response, subject/background separation, depth and, for video actually viewed, cadence and deformation. Name the source and inspection range. A still cannot establish motion behavior.
2. **Inferred implementation:** list possible explanations with uncertainty. Bloom could come from optics, grading or rendering; shallow apparent depth does not establish a lens model. Do not assert exact lens, filter, renderer, lighting kit or training method without production evidence.
3. **Chosen project design:** select functional relationships that serve this work and its approved family. Preserve invariants and define allowable shot deltas. This is a design proposal, not a claim about how the reference was made, and does not overwrite an approved look.
4. **Validation shot:** state what an authorized test would need to show at normal speed and across a cut; reuse sufficient existing evidence. Do not generate a test automatically. Record untested dimensions rather than treating the written recipe as validated.

Example: “Face remains readable against a saturated background; cloth has a broader, duller highlight than metal” is an observable relationship. “Shot with a particular cinema camera and diffusion filter” is an inference unless evidenced. “Keep facial values readable while assigning different responses to cloth/metal” is a transferable design choice; it applies differently to photographic, 2D, 3D and other approved families, without imposing realistic specular highlights on flat illustration.

Do not transfer a tutorial's mandatory aspect ratio, stock weather, repeated prop, national-style label or camera brand as a rule for unrelated stories. Choose the smallest useful constraints, not an adjective inventory. Local success remains local until reviewed and approved for broader use.

## Compare the actual production chain

When learning from a canvas, compare the relevant brief/storyboard, selected input images or motion controls, submitted prompt/settings when available, actual output and editorial selection. A short motion prompt may rely on a carefully composed input; a long prompt may describe choices the result never executes. Compare total design/control information, not word count or camera-brand vocabulary.

Canvas nodes are mutable: a current prompt, nested settings, top-level settings and an attached older output can disagree. Record that uncertainty; do not reconstruct a historical generation request from whichever field supports the preferred explanation. Treat duration conflicts, contradictory camera instructions and failed references as counterevidence, even in an attractive example. Transfer a useful relationship in original words, not an entire author's template, character design or unverified recipe. Cross-genre examples support design options, not proof that an advertisement's pace or a trailer's density fits a dramatic episode.

## Environment layers and material hierarchy

Where spatial readability matters, separate the environment's fixed structure from changing dressing, light and atmosphere. State which anchors must stay fixed across angles, and which layer actually moves. Subject motion, camera motion, atmospheric motion and foreground occlusion are not interchangeable.

Use a foreground edge, middle-distance action or background landmark only when it helps orientation, attention, mood or a reveal. A static camera can gain depth from a passing subject; a moving camera can reveal parallax among fixed anchors. Do not make the whole world drift with the subject or require three busy depth layers in every shot. A flat graphic composition or clean empty frame may be the intended design.

Example: a doorway edge briefly hides the arriving listener, then reveals their distance from the seated speaker; the room's rear window stays fixed. This is a spatial/attention relationship, not a request for arbitrary foliage, particles or constant camera travel. Its acting and reveal timing belong to direction.

Define light/color hierarchy at the required level: the audience's first attention target, what may compete, and which surface or silhouette carries separation. Check whether a general style clause contradicts a local approved face, wardrobe, material or lighting delta before passing the contract to generation.
