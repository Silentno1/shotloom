# Whole-body emotional performance

Use for emotional expression, gaze, listening and body acting, including joy, anger, fear, affection, shame, disappointment, pride, uncertainty, mixed feelings and concealment. These are situations to interpret, not an emotion-to-gesture dictionary. Emotion words may orient intent but cannot alone specify a material performance beat.

## Design the playable event

Start from what the character knows, wants from someone or something, and is currently doing. Separate inner experience, the outward impression they intend, and what the audience should actually notice. Preserve ambiguity when the story requires it; do not invent hidden motives or expose a reveal early.

For a material beat, record a compact `performance_contract` in the director package, using only relevant decisions:

- Entering posture, activity and attention; immediate objective and perceived trigger.
- Development: sustain, intensify, recede, conceal, redirect, mix or another justified course. No mandatory escalation, breakdown or recovery.
- Outward intensity described in visible terms, with a readable minimum and a contextual ceiling. For example, a listener must register new information but must not reveal their concealed allegiance. Do not use arbitrary numerical emotion scores.
- Selected gaze, facial, breath/voice and body cues; their overlap or order and relation to a line, discovery, contact or other event. Use exact timing only when materially locked; do not stretch a response to fill generation duration.
- Observable endpoint and any carryover across cuts; required cues versus optional natural variation; shot visibility and review criteria.

This is a semantic handoff, not a new platform field requirement or a mandatory form for empty scenery. Keep it as brief as the beat permits. A static portrait specifies a selected state, not a claimed performed transition.

## Attention and gaze

Name a spatially grounded attention target and why it changes or deliberately stays. Where material, describe acquiring the target, sustaining attention, breaking or returning to it, and whether eyes lead, follow or stay independent of the head. Ground offscreen targets in established space.

Distinguish visible eye direction from the character's inferred thought. An unchanging gaze can carry a deliberate refusal; random eye wandering and repeated blinking do not constitute emotional development. Eye avoidance does not prove lying, eye contact does not prove honesty or affection, and pupil size is not a universal emotional control.

Coordinate lids, brows, cheeks, mouth and jaw only to the degree needed for a readable whole expression. Do not treat a smile, wet eyes or a furrowed brow as sufficient evidence of the intended feeling. Do not require facial symmetry, continuous facial motion or a separate instruction for every muscle.

## Body, breath and interaction

Choose support, weight, torso orientation, shoulder/neck tension, hand activity, approach/withdrawal or touch from the character's task and relationship. State meaningful changes and their consequences, not decorative hand motion. Preserve planted support and existing object contact unless the beat changes them.

Let breath, speech, face and body have motivated timing differences. They can reinforce each other or deliberately conflict, as when polite words accompany withdrawal; unexplained conflict is an error. Do not make every channel peak together. Listening characters continue to process what is available to them; they must not react to information before hearing or seeing it. No fixed reaction delay applies to all events.

Select the smallest sufficient set of cues, not an arbitrary cue count. Quiet attention may be enough in one scene; exuberance, panic or stylized comedy may need large whole-body changes in another. Neither restrained realism nor maximum intensity is the default for every performance.

## Ensemble attention and interaction

For a material group beat, identify the initiator, direct receiver and relevant witnesses. Ground who can see/hear the trigger, what each is already doing, whose attention moves next and which response must be readable. Give supporting people continuing tasks or purposeful stillness; do not make everyone repeatedly nod, grin or turn toward the camera.

Let response order follow access to information and relationships, not a compulsory stagger measured in identical delays. Example: one person struggles to pass a cup while holding a bag; the nearby partner frees a hand to receive it; a distracted witness notices only when addressed. Track the contact/handoff and shifting eyelines, rather than issuing three simultaneous “natural reactions.” Do not invent these events if they are absent from the authorized scene.

Synchrony can be correct for a shared sudden sound, practiced movement, collective ritual or stylized comedy. Specify that reason and the approved scale; do not prohibit synchrony as inherently artificial. Likewise, asynchronous motion is not automatically credible. Keep a primary attention owner in each important phase and avoid equally emphatic gestures obscuring the focal action. Show only the cues readable at the chosen framing; preserve individual endpoints across cuts.

## Physical expression details, only when relevant

For visible tears, sweat, flushing, trembling, laughter, breathlessness or similar phenomena, specify the story-relevant onset, evolution, interaction and residual state. Do not require these as proof of emotion or add symptoms to intensify an otherwise sufficient beat.

For example, if a moving tear is important, distinguish existing wetness from overflow, travel and any wipe/contact; preserve visible wet traces across a continuous cut. If laughter matters, distinguish its onset, interaction with breath/speech and settling from a fixed open-mouth pose. These are conditional examples, not mandatory sequences or physiology guarantees. Stylized shape changes, symbols and material behavior follow the approved medium and performance scale.

## Character continuity beyond one beat

For recurring characters, derive a small performance baseline from approved characterization: habitual task/attention behavior, relational distance, voice identity and permitted expressive range. Distinguish stable identity from fatigue, injury, status, intimacy or emotional residue specific to this scene. Do not reuse identical gestures to certify identity, or assign habits from demographic stereotypes.

Across consequential scenes, track changes in what the person wants from others, what they can reveal and which earlier experience still affects their behavior. A later scene may suppress, redirect or release an earlier emotion rather than reset or escalate it. Compare contrasting relationships; the same character need not use the same tempo or eye contact with a superior and a trusted partner.

Play dialogue as an attempt on someone—reassure, evade, test, bargain, challenge or another story-supported tactic—not an emotion adjective applied to every line. Let tactics change when the other person's response changes their prospects. Exact words stay locked; subtext cannot invent motives or expose a secret prematurely. Voice identity persists while pace, phrasing, tension and volume vary with the playable event. Carry only differences relevant to this scene, not a biography in every prompt.

## Framing, medium and handoff

For consequential dialogue delivery, separate stable voice identity, the scene's interpersonal tactic and the audible execution. Choose only relevant stress, phrasing, pause/interruption, pace, volume, breath or line ending; anchor a change to the actual response/discovery rather than assigning one voice preset to the whole atmosphere. Preserve exact wording and who is being addressed. A firm refusal may remain ordinary in volume; an urgent call may need projection for distance, not generic anger. These are contextual options, not fixed mappings. Coordinate the chosen delivery with gaze/body and the listener's access; do not add a gesture for every stressed word. [director-decision-practice.md](director-decision-practice.md) supplies the joint decision and paper-comparison rules.

Judge visibility at the intended shot scale and playback speed. Do not rely on a tiny eye change in an unreadable wide shot. Within camera authority, choose readable framing or an appropriate larger cue; flag an exact-lock conflict instead of silently changing it. Avoid busy camera motion that obscures the required reaction.

Keep intent, causal order and approved intensity across live-action, 2D, 3D, three-render-two, stop-motion or custom styles. Their amplitude, pose holds, deformation and cadence may differ. Do not impose realistic facial detail on limited animation or force a micro-expression into every medium. Character-specific habits come from approved characterization, not demographic stereotypes.

Pass the `performance_contract` to generation with the source version. Mark which visible changes are essential, which are optional and which are forbidden substitutions. Include the corresponding normal-playback review question: can the audience read the intended response without losing the intended ambiguity? Breath or mouth movement does not grant permission to generate its sound; preserve the separate sound-ownership plan.

## Contrasting application probes

Use these to challenge a proposed design, not as stock acting recipes:

| Situation | Decision that must change with context | Failure to catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Private delight versus polite congratulations | Genuine release versus maintained social presentation, as established by the scene | The same broad grin and nod loop for both |
| Controlled anger versus open confrontation | What the person wants to achieve and how much they reveal | Mandatory shouting, fists or a compulsory subtle leak |
| Attraction while listening versus watching for danger | Attention target, reason for sustained attention and resulting action | Identical staring described with different emotion adjectives |
| Concealed disappointment versus open grief | Who may notice, degree of control and visible endpoint | Automatic tears or an invented emotional breakdown |
| Relief with continuing fear | Which pressure eases and which remains | Face/body instantly resetting to cheerful neutrality |
| Stylized celebration versus deliberate stillness | Approved medium, visibility and performance scale | Flattening exuberance or adding meaningless motion to a hold |

A successful written contract is design evidence only. Model execution and emotional credibility require actual-media review.
